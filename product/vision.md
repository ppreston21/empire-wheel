# Empire Wheel Vision

## Product Thesis

Empire Wheel is an interactive learning platform for understanding how civilizations acquire, organize, exercise, and transfer power.

World history is organized as a wheel of hegemonic civilizations. Each spoke represents a civilization or hegemonic system; the transitions between spokes represent the historical handoff of power.

Learners move through the wheel chronologically, investigating not only what happened, but why one system became dominant, how it functioned, what evidence survives, and why power eventually moved elsewhere.

## The Experience

Empire Wheel is not a textbook divided into webpages.

Each civilization is a substantial guided curriculum composed of approximately ten modules. Each module should provide roughly 2–5 hours of meaningful engagement through a combination of:

* original historical narrative
* academic scholarship
* archaeological and primary evidence
* maps and spatial exploration
* images and reconstructions
* embedded lectures and video
* timelines
* economic and institutional visualizations
* historical argument exercises
* AI-assisted critique
* study guides and review

A learner should repeatedly move between narrative, evidence, visualization, and interpretation.

## Core Learning Loop

Every module follows the same broad progression:

**Orient → Learn → Read → See → Explore → Examine → Argue → Review → Study**

The exact content may vary by historical period, but the intellectual process remains recognizable.

### Orient

Establish the period, geography, major actors, and central historical question.

### Learn

Provide a coherent narrative explaining the events and systems necessary to understand the module.

### Read

Direct learners toward selected scholarship and accessible academic material.

### See

Use artifacts, archaeological sites, reconstructions, diagrams, photographs, and other visual evidence to make the civilization tangible.

### Explore

Allow learners to investigate geography, settlements, trade networks, military campaigns, territorial expansion, and other spatial relationships.

### Examine

Present historical evidence and ask what conclusions the evidence does and does not support.

Evidence may include texts, inscriptions, administrative records, archaeological sites, artifacts, iconography, maps, or quantitative information.

### Argue

Require the learner to make a historical claim and support it with evidence.

### Review

Provide structured feedback evaluating historical accuracy, use of evidence, reasoning, uncertainty, and unsupported inference.

### Study

Conclude with a persistent study guide containing the chronology, concepts, people, places, evidence, debates, and questions the learner should now understand.

## The Empire Wheel

The top-level interface represents the historical curriculum as a wheel.

Each spoke represents a civilization or hegemonic period.

Selecting a spoke opens its curriculum.

Progress through that curriculum is reflected directly on the wheel.

Transitions between civilizations are first-class historical subjects rather than empty chronological boundaries. Empire Wheel should continually ask:

**Why did the baton move?**

* geography
* population and production
* surplus and wealth
* political institutions
* religion and legitimacy
* military organization
* trade and transportation
* information and administration
* technology
* causes of hegemonic ascent and decline

## Visual History

Empire Wheel should make historical systems visible.

Important recurring visual experiences include:

### Places

Maps, city plans, archaeological sites, reconstructions, and important landscapes.

### Movement

Trade routes, migrations, military campaigns, territorial expansion, and communication networks.

### Production

Interactive representations of how food, labor, commodities, taxation, and wealth moved through a society.

### Power

Visual models showing which institutions controlled resources and how economic capacity became political, religious, or military power.

These visual systems should be reusable across civilizations so learners can compare structures separated by thousands of years.

## Scholarship and Sources

Empire Wheel should connect learners directly with serious historical scholarship rather than hide scholarship behind simplified summaries.

Where practical, modules should link to accessible academic papers, books, museum collections, archives, university resources, translations, lectures, and other high-quality external material.

Empire Wheel must distinguish between:

1. what surviving evidence directly shows,
2. how scholars interpret that evidence,
3. competing scholarly interpretations, and
4. Empire Wheel's own synthesis or argument.

The application must never invent quotations, citations, artifacts, translations, or historical evidence.

## Learning Outcome

Completing a module should mean more than reaching the bottom of a webpage.

A learner should leave able to:

* explain the central historical sequence,
* locate the civilization geographically and chronologically,
* recognize important evidence,
* explain competing interpretations,
* connect institutions to economic and military power,
* make a defensible historical argument,
* identify uncertainty in that argument, and
* explain how the module contributes to the larger hegemonic transition represented by the Empire Wheel.

## Initial Product

The first complete curriculum is Ancient Mesopotamia.

It will establish the content model, learning loop, visual grammar, evidence system, progress system, and study experience that subsequent civilization spokes reuse.

Version 0.1 should prove one thing:

**A learner can enter a historical period knowing relatively little and emerge several hours later capable of making and defending a substantially more sophisticated historical argument.**

## Product Principle

**Don't just consume history. Interrogate it.**

Narrative provides the story.

Evidence constrains the story.

Comparison reveals the system.

Argument demonstrates understanding.


# The Empire Wheel

Empire Wheel follows nineteen successive centers of hegemonic power from the emergence of the first cities to the modern world.

Each spoke is a ten-module curriculum examining a civilization at three levels:

* **Inheritance:** the world it received from its predecessors.
* **System:** how geography, production, institutions, technology, culture, and military power combined to sustain dominance.
* **Handoff:** why that system weakened, transformed, or was surpassed, and what the next hegemonic civilization inherited from it.

The wheel is not intended to imply that only one important civilization existed at any moment. Each spoke instead identifies a civilization whose institutions, productive capacity, military organization, commercial networks, or cultural systems exerted unusually broad influence over the world connected to it.

---

## 1. Sumer — The First Urban System

**Uruk and the Early Dynastic World**

Sumer begins the Empire Wheel with the transformation of agricultural settlements into cities.

Its curriculum examines the emergence of irrigation agriculture, urban specialization, monumental institutions, administrative recordkeeping, writing, organized warfare, long-distance trade, and the city-state.

The central question is:

**How did human communities become complex enough to require the first cities, governments, bureaucracies, and written systems of administration?**

The spoke begins in the world inherited from the Ubaid period and ends with politically fragmented Sumerian cities confronting a new possibility: territorial empire.

**Handoff:** Sumer → Akkad

---

## 2. Akkad — The First Territorial Empire

Akkad represents a fundamental change in political scale.

Rather than a network dominated by independent city-states, the Akkadian rulers attempted to place numerous cities, peoples, trade routes, and productive regions beneath a single imperial authority.

The curriculum examines Sargon and his successors, military expansion, imperial administration, political legitimacy, linguistic and cultural integration, trade networks, and the practical limits of governing territory.

The central question is:

**What changes when political power attempts to control not merely a city, but an entire system of cities?**

Its collapse exposes the difficulty of sustaining centralized authority across a large and diverse territory.

**Handoff:** Akkad → Ur III

---

## 3. Ur III — The Administrative State

The Third Dynasty of Ur represents one of antiquity's most extraordinary experiments in centralized administration.

The spoke investigates the reconstruction of southern Mesopotamian political authority after Akkad, the extraordinary proliferation of administrative records, taxation, labor organization, agricultural management, redistribution, law, and state bureaucracy.

The central question is:

**How much of an economy can a government actually see, record, organize, and control?**

Ur III demonstrates both the power and the fragility of intensive centralized administration.

**Handoff:** Ur III → Old Babylonian World

---

## 4. Old Babylon — Law, Kingship, and the Mesopotamian City

The Old Babylonian period explores a world of competing kingdoms in which political authority was reconstructed around cities, dynasties, trade, diplomacy, and law.

Babylon's rise under Hammurabi provides the focal point, but the curriculum extends beyond one king or one famous law code.

The spoke examines household economics, merchants, property, debt, temples, diplomacy, warfare, law, literacy, and royal ideology.

The central question is:

**How does a ruler create durable authority in a competitive world of cities, families, merchants, temples, and rival kingdoms?**

**Handoff:** Old Babylonian order → the larger territorial powers of the Late Bronze Age

---

## 5. Hittites — The Great-Power System

The Hittite Empire introduces learners to the interconnected diplomatic world of the Late Bronze Age.

Its strength depended not simply on conquest but upon diplomacy, treaties, vassalage, military organization, control of Anatolian territory, and participation in an international system linking Egypt, Mesopotamia, Syria, and the eastern Mediterranean.

The central question is:

**What happens when several powerful states must compete, negotiate, trade, marry, and fight within the same international system?**

The spoke culminates in the breakdown of the Late Bronze Age political order.

**Handoff:** Bronze Age great powers → the Iron Age imperial world

---

## 6. Assyria — The Machinery of Empire

Assyria develops territorial empire into an extraordinarily powerful political and military system.

The curriculum follows the Middle and Neo-Assyrian traditions while concentrating on the institutions that made Assyrian expansion possible: professionalized warfare, siege technology, roads, provincial administration, tribute, deportation, intelligence, monumental propaganda, and deliberate demonstrations of royal power.

The central question is:

**What institutions are required to turn repeated conquest into a durable imperial machine?**

Assyria becomes a model of both imperial effectiveness and imperial overextension.

**Handoff:** Assyria → Neo-Babylon

---

## 7. Neo-Babylon — The Imperial Metropolis

The Neo-Babylonian Empire inherits much of the political world created by Assyria but centers power on Babylon and the wealth of southern Mesopotamia.

The curriculum examines Nebuchadnezzar II, monumental construction, urban life, temples, conquest, deportation, commerce, astronomy, and Babylon's position at the center of an imperial economy.

The central question is:

**How does an imperial capital turn accumulated wealth, religion, labor, and spectacle into political legitimacy?**

Its relatively brief dominance ends when an even larger imperial system absorbs Mesopotamia.

**Handoff:** Babylon → Persia

---

## 8. Persia — Empire at Continental Scale

The Achaemenid Persian Empire brings an unprecedented expanse of peoples, languages, economies, and ecological regions into a single political system.

The curriculum examines Cyrus, Darius, satrapies, taxation, roads, imperial communication, military organization, local autonomy, royal ideology, monumental architecture, and the integration of regional elites.

The central question is:

**How can an empire govern enormous diversity without making every province identical?**

Persia establishes an imperial model that its conquerors would inherit rather than simply destroy.

**Handoff:** Persia → Macedonian and Hellenistic World

---

## 9. Greece — The Hellenistic Network

The Greek spoke begins with the competitive city-state world but culminates in the Macedonian conquests and the enormous Hellenistic cultural and commercial sphere that followed Alexander.

The curriculum examines the polis, colonization, warfare, citizenship, philosophy, markets, Macedonian state formation, Alexander's conquests, successor kingdoms, and the diffusion of Greek language and institutions across older Near Eastern civilizations.

The central question is:

**How can cultural influence become larger and more durable than the political state that originally carried it?**

The Hellenistic world becomes the environment within which Rome expands eastward.

**Handoff:** Greece and the Hellenistic kingdoms → Rome

---

## 10. Rome — The Mediterranean System

Rome transforms a city-state into a republic, a territorial empire, and ultimately an integrated Mediterranean political system.

Its curriculum examines republican institutions, citizenship, conquest, slavery, infrastructure, taxation, trade, law, urbanism, military organization, imperial government, cultural assimilation, Christianity, and the pressures that transformed the empire.

The central question is:

**How did Rome convert conquest into one of history's most durable systems of political, legal, economic, and cultural integration?**

The western empire fragments, while the eastern Roman system survives and evolves.

**Handoff:** Classical Rome → Byzantium

---

## 11. Byzantium — The Roman State Rebuilt

Byzantium asks what happens when an ancient imperial system survives by changing.

The eastern Roman Empire preserved Roman government and law while developing a distinctly medieval political, military, religious, and commercial order centered on Constantinople.

The curriculum examines Justinian, Christianity, imperial administration, Mediterranean trade, frontier warfare, diplomacy, taxation, military adaptation, iconoclasm, and Constantinople's extraordinary strategic position.

The central question is:

**How does a civilization preserve continuity while repeatedly reinventing the institutions that made continuity possible?**

Its greatest early challenge comes from a new imperial civilization emerging from Arabia.

**Handoff:** Byzantine-Persian world → Islamic Caliphates

---

## 12. Islamic Caliphates — The Afro-Eurasian Crossroads

The early Islamic conquests rapidly reorganized much of the political world stretching from the Mediterranean through Persia and eventually into Central Asia.

The curriculum follows the Rashidun, Umayyad, and especially Abbasid imperial systems while examining religion, administration, taxation, trade, urbanism, scholarship, translation, law, finance, agriculture, and the enormous commercial networks connecting Africa, Europe, the Middle East, India, and Asia.

The central question is:

**What happens when political expansion, commercial integration, and a shared intellectual language connect regions that had previously belonged to competing imperial systems?**

Political unity eventually fragments even while the wider Islamic cultural and commercial world remains powerful.

**Handoff:** Caliphal system → Mongol Eurasia

---

## 13. Mongols — The Empire of Movement

The Mongol Empire creates the largest contiguous land empire in history by turning mobility itself into a source of strategic power.

Its curriculum examines steppe ecology, mounted warfare, intelligence, logistics, conquest, terror, religious pluralism, administration, tribute, trade, the postal network, successor khanates, and the intensification of Eurasian exchange.

The central question is:

**How can a society with relatively few people dominate enormous populations and reshape connections across an entire continent?**

The Mongol conquests destroy some states while creating the conditions from which others emerge.

**Handoff:** Mongol Eurasia → Ming China and the early modern world

---

## 14. Ming China — The Productive Giant

Ming China represents one of the largest concentrations of population, manufacturing capacity, agricultural production, urban life, administrative sophistication, and commercial activity in the premodern world.

The curriculum examines the reconstruction of Chinese rule after the Yuan, the civil bureaucracy, agriculture, cities, manufacturing, monetary systems, maritime expeditions, frontier defense, internal commerce, global silver flows, and China's place in the emerging early-modern economy.

The central question is:

**What does hegemonic power look like when immense productive capacity matters as much as territorial conquest?**

Meanwhile, maritime states on China's distant western horizon begin building a new type of global network.

**Handoff:** Asian-centered commercial systems → Iberian oceanic expansion

---

## 15. Iberians — The First Oceanic Empires

Portugal and Spain make the ocean a permanent highway of imperial power.

The spoke examines navigation, shipbuilding, Atlantic islands, West African trade, the conquest of the Americas, colonial administration, missionary activity, forced labor, slavery, silver, plantations, the Columbian Exchange, and the first enduring connections among the world's major oceanic regions.

The central question is:

**How did control of ocean routes transform regional kingdoms into global empires?**

The Iberian empires create the infrastructure of global exchange but struggle to monopolize it.

**Handoff:** Iberian maritime empire → Dutch commercial hegemony

---

## 16. Dutch Republic — The Commercial Empire

The Dutch Republic represents a shift from conquest financed by states toward global power increasingly organized through merchants, corporations, finance, shipping, and capital markets.

The curriculum examines the VOC, Amsterdam, commercial institutions, banking, joint-stock finance, maritime technology, colonial violence, Asian trade, logistics, information networks, and the Dutch Golden Age.

The central question is:

**What happens when the institutions of commerce themselves become instruments of geopolitical power?**

The Dutch system helps create a commercial world that larger territorial states learn to exploit.

**Handoff:** Dutch commercial system → France and Britain

---

## 17. France — The Centralized Continental State

France demonstrates the enormous potential of population, centralized administration, taxation, military organization, culture, and state power in early modern Europe.

The curriculum examines Bourbon monarchy, Versailles, bureaucracy, military expansion, colonial competition, mercantilism, demographic strength, intellectual culture, fiscal constraints, revolution, and Napoleon.

The central question is:

**How much power can a state generate by concentrating the people and resources of a large territorial society?**

France repeatedly approaches European dominance but ultimately helps create the conditions for a different hegemonic model centered on industry, finance, and the sea.

**Handoff:** French continental challenge → British global system

---

## 18. Britain — The Industrial and Maritime System

Britain combines maritime supremacy, global commerce, financial institutions, colonial networks, industrialization, scientific capacity, and a rapidly expanding productive economy.

The curriculum examines the Royal Navy, empire, Atlantic commerce, banking, parliamentary government, coal, mechanization, industrial cities, railways, global trade, India, communications, and the political economy of the nineteenth century.

The central question is:

**How did industrial production and command of global networks transform the scale of hegemonic power?**

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Britain's own system helps create a larger industrial competitor across the Atlantic.

**Handoff:** British world system → United States

---

## 19. United States — The Network Hegemon

The United States inherits an industrial, financial, maritime, and institutional world partly constructed under British leadership and expands it to unprecedented scale.

The curriculum examines continental development, industrialization, mass production, immigration, corporations, technological innovation, finance, energy, two world wars, military logistics, global institutions, the dollar system, consumer culture, computing, communications, and the postwar international order.

The central question is:

**What does hegemony become when productive capacity, technological systems, military reach, finance, culture, and information networks operate simultaneously at global scale?**

Unlike earlier spokes, its handoff cannot yet be taught as settled history.

The final modules therefore ask a different question:

**Which elements of American hegemony remain durable, which are weakening, and what would evidence of the next handoff actually look like?**

---

# The Recurring Question of the Wheel

Across all nineteen spokes, Empire Wheel returns to the same fundamental questions:

**What creates surplus?**

**Who controls it?**

**How is it converted into political and military power?**

**Which institutions allow that power to scale?**

**What information must the system collect to govern itself?**

**What networks connect the hegemon to the rest of the world?**

**What makes the system legitimate to the people who participate in it?**

**What eventually prevents the system from adapting?**

And finally:

**Why does the baton move?**
